description: "Rehearse the pipeline interview — win rate denominators, cohort funnels, speed-to-lead causality, auditing a lead score, stuck-deal definitions, discount creep, and the MQL argument."
interview:
  - q: "How would you build a stage conversion funnel?"
    a: "As a cohort, not a snapshot. A snapshot counts what's sitting in each stage right now, which isn't a conversion rate at all — in a growing pipeline it makes late-stage conversion look terrible simply because deals haven't got there yet. So I'd take opportunities created in a period and follow them, counting how many ever reached each stage rather than where they are now, because a deal currently in negotiation passed through discovery and counting only current stage understates every early stage. Then I'd exclude cohorts younger than one sales-cycle length, and do that per segment, since 28 days for SMB and 186 for strategic are completely different windows. And I'd build it twice — by count and by value — because the stage that loses the most deals is usually not the stage that loses the most money, and only the value funnel shows where revenue actually leaks."
  - q: "Our win rate is 40%. What's wrong with that number?"
    a: "Probably the denominator and definitely the mix. Win rate should be closed-won over closed deals only; if open opportunities are in the denominator you're counting live pipeline as losses. But the bigger issue is that it's blended across deal types — renewals win around 88% and new business around 33%, so a combined figure is mostly a statement about the renewal book rather than about selling. Then segment: self-serve wins about 44% and strategic about 24%, so the blended number is an artefact of this quarter's mix and will move when the mix moves, with nobody's selling having changed. I'd report win rate filtered to new business, split by segment, and on a trailing-twelve-month basis so quarterly noise doesn't drive conclusions."
  - q: "You see that leads touched within an hour convert three times better. Would you set a one-hour SLA?"
    a: "I'd want to rule out reverse causation first, because the obvious alternative explanation is that reps call the best leads fastest — in which case speed is a symptom of lead quality rather than a cause of conversion. The test is to repeat the analysis within a single score band, and ideally within a single touch-count bucket: if fast-touched A-band leads still convert better than slow-touched A-band leads, the effect is real and an SLA is justified. I'd also check whether fast touches cluster in one region or shift, which would mean I'm measuring coverage rather than speed. Once it survives that, I'd derive the target from our own curve rather than importing a number from a blog post, and present the curve alongside the SLA so the team sees where it came from — otherwise it's an arbitrary stick and gets gamed."
  - q: "How would you tell whether a lead score is any good?"
    a: "Plot conversion rate by score band and check it increases monotonically — A above B above C above D. That's the whole test, and a surprising number of production scores fail it. I'd put lead volume next to each bar, because a band with thirty leads and a high rate is noise. Then I'd quantify the lift as the A-band rate over the overall rate; under about 1.3x it isn't worth reps' attention. Two confounds to control for, both of which inflate the score's apparent value: reps work high-scoring leads harder, so I'd facet by touch count, and low bands may never be routed at all, so their rate reflects neglect rather than quality. Then the useful part — test individual fields against conversion and compare. At a developer-first company a single behavioural field like 'has made a first API call' usually outperforms the whole demographic model, because it's evidence of intent rather than a guess about it."
  - q: "Marketing says 1,870 MQLs, sales says 860 accepted. How do you handle that meeting?"
    a: "I'd put both numbers on one screen with their definitions attached and the funnel between them, because half the conflict is that nobody has ever seen the two figures side by side with the cohorting rule stated. Both sides have to cohort by MQL date or the monthly rates can never tie. Then the diagnostic chart: rejection rate by source. If rejection is concentrated in one or two sources it's a targeting problem and you fix the channel; if it's uniform across every source, marketing's qualification bar is simply lower than sales' acceptance bar and you fix the definition. I'd also check whether rejection rate tracks leads-per-rep, because if it rises when the team gets busier, the leads weren't worse — capacity was — and that's a routing finding wearing a quality complaint as a disguise. The chart I'd really want is win rate by original score band on accepted leads only, which turns 'are these leads good' into 'does the score add information after the human filter'."
  - q: "What makes a deal 'stuck'?"
    a: "Not a fixed number of days, because technical validation legitimately takes longer than discovery and a single global threshold flags every healthy enterprise deal. I'd compute each stage's median days from closed historical deals, per segment, and flag deals exceeding roughly twice their own stage median. Then I'd add the signals that are specific to selling AI infrastructure: a late-stage deal with no completed POC, an enterprise deal where the security review hasn't started, a deal over a quarter of a million with only one contact engaged, and any deal past its own close date. I'd combine those into one composite boolean in the recipe so the action list is sortable rather than six separate columns nobody cross-references. And a blank next step on a large late-stage deal is itself a finding worth surfacing."
  - q: "How would you measure whether discounting is working?"
    a: "Win rate by discount band, and I'd present it as a question rather than a conclusion. Often the relationship is flat or an inverted U, which suggests heavy discounts cluster on deals that were already lost and the discount was a rescue attempt rather than a lever. That's correlational and I'd say so. The analysis I'd be more confident about needs quote version history: average discount by version number, which at Namilio climbs roughly two and a half points per re-quote, so a deal that goes four rounds ends about ten points worse. That's invisible if the system only keeps the final quote. I'd also compute post-discount gross margin per line by joining to the product's margin, because a 25% discount on a 41%-margin usage product leaves around 21%, and on a product with real infrastructure cost behind it that may be below water. Finally I'd count approval days as a cycle cost, since they sit inside the sales cycle and nobody counts that half."
  - q: "How do you decide between inbound and outbound investment?"
    a: "I'd refuse to answer it company-wide, because neither wins overall — each wins a segment, and a blended answer is wrong whichever way it comes out. The deliverable is a segment-by-motion heat map coloured by closed-won: product-led dominates self-serve and SMB, outbound dominates enterprise and strategic, and mid-market is genuinely contested, which is why the argument always lives there. Before any of that I'd split product-led out of inbound, since a developer who read the docs and called the API is nothing like a webinar form fill, and lumping them together lets PLG's good conversion hide inbound marketing's weaker conversion. I'd compare on rate, deal size, cycle length and cost per closed dollar rather than lead volume. And I'd raise both teams' best objections myself — that outbound leads were often warmed by marketing first, checkable via first-touch channel, and that inbound would decay without brand spend, which is probably true and unmeasurable on this data."
  - q: "What's sales velocity useful for?"
    a: "Less as a number than as a decomposition. It's opportunities times deal size times win rate, divided by cycle days, so when someone says 'we need more pipeline' it turns a vague demand into a choice between four measurable levers — and more opportunities is usually the slowest and most expensive of the four. The one people undervalue is cycle length, because it compounds: halving the cycle doubles revenue per day, and a lot of cycle time turns out to be process rather than selling, like approval days and unscoped POCs, which are cheap to fix. Deal size via multi-product attach is often the next cheapest. The critical caveat is never to compute it blended across segments — dividing strategic's deal size by self-serve's cycle length produces a number that describes no business that exists."
  - q: "A VP wants a rep leaderboard on the pipeline health dashboard. What do you say?"
    a: "I'd build it, on a different dashboard. The moment a diagnostic dashboard becomes an evaluative one, reps start managing the dashboard instead of the pipeline — deals get parked in early stages, close dates get pushed in bulk, and the stage data that every other chart depends on degrades. So I'd keep pipeline health diagnostic and put ranking on a coaching dashboard with the fairness controls that ranking requires: filter or flag ramping reps, normalise by segment since quotas and deal sizes differ by a factor of ten, and use trailing twelve months rather than a quarter so one large deal doesn't decide the order. I'd explain the reasoning rather than just refusing, because the VP's underlying question — who needs help — is completely legitimate and better served by a dashboard designed for it."

# Pipeline Analytics: Interview Questions

Ten questions on modules 9's material. The pattern here is different from the demand module: these test whether you will **check the denominator and the confound** before answering.

::note{icon="i-lucide-mic"}
Pipeline numbers can be reconciled, so interviewers probe for rigour rather than for honesty about uncertainty. Almost every strong answer above names a filter (Type, Segment, cohort age) or a confound (reverse causation, survivorship) unprompted.
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
**Three moves that carry these answers.** Filter to New Business and one segment before quoting any rate. Cohort by creation date and exclude immature cohorts. When shown a correlation, name the reverse-causation story and say how you would test it.
::
