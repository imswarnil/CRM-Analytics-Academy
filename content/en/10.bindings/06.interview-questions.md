interview:
  - q: "What is a binding, and how is it different from faceting?"
    a: "Faceting is automatic between widgets on the same dataset — click one, the others filter. A binding is explicit wiring: I take either what the viewer selected or what a query returned, and feed it into another widget's query, title or visibility. Faceting is behaviour you get; a binding is behaviour you configure, and pay for."
  - q: "What is the first binding you add to a dashboard?"
    a: "A dynamic title carrying the current selection. It costs minutes, it never breaks, and it fixes the most expensive ambiguity in analytics communication — a filtered screenshot being read as the whole picture. I assume every widget will eventually be pasted into a message with no context, because eventually one is."
  - q: "Selection binding or results binding — what is the practical difference?"
    a: "A selection binding reads what somebody clicked, so the input is visible on screen and it is debuggable by looking. A results binding reads a value another query returned — a company average, a max, a top category — which is more powerful and much harder to debug, because the input is invisible. It also creates an execution order: the dependent widget cannot start until the source returns, so two parallel queries become two sequential ones."
  - q: "A dashboard is slow in a way its widget count does not explain. What do you check?"
    a: "Chained results bindings. Each one serialises what would otherwise run in parallel, so three in a chain means the last widget waits on two round trips. Nothing on screen suggests the dependency exists. After that I check whether widgets that could share a query each have their own, and whether anything is aggregating a very fine grain on every load."
  - q: "What is the risk with heavy binding use?"
    a: "It is logic stored in widget configuration — unreadable at a glance, undiffable, and documented nowhere anybody looks. A dashboard with twenty of them works beautifully for its author and becomes untouchable for everyone else, so the next person builds a new dashboard beside it rather than risking a change. That is how an organisation ends up with four pipeline dashboards and an argument about which is right. The failure is organisational rather than technical."
  - q: "How do you keep one maintainable?"
    a: "Three habits. One line per binding in the dashboard description, saying what feeds what. Prefer breadth over depth — five widgets bound to one filter is far better than three bound in a chain, even though it is more bindings. And name queries for what they contain rather than query_7, because binding references are the only readable part of the arrangement. The test I apply is whether a colleague could add a widget next month without asking me."
  - q: "When would you not use a binding at all?"
    a: "When the computed value is a company metric — that belongs in the recipe, computed once, rather than recalculated for every viewer on every load in a place nobody can find. And when the binding exists to cram two audiences onto one screen; that is two dashboards wearing a trench coat, and splitting them makes both simpler to read and easier to share."
  - q: "What edge cases do you test?"
    a: "Nothing selected, one thing selected, several things selected — three clicks, and it catches nearly every binding bug. For dependent filters I also test the stale selection: pick a rep, change the region so that rep is out of scope, and check the dashboard clears it rather than silently filtering to somebody invisible. And for results bindings, the empty source, because a query that legitimately returns nothing is often the interesting case."

# Bindings: Interview Questions

Binding questions are maintainability questions in disguise. Anyone can describe the mechanism; the signal is whether you know what it costs.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The answer that separates people** is the one about the organisational failure — that an over-bound dashboard leads to a duplicate being built rather than to a change being made. That is not a thing you read; it is a thing you have watched happen.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"Faceting is behaviour you get; a binding is behaviour you configure and pay for."**
2. **"Results bindings serialise execution — that is the invisible slowness."**
3. **"If a colleague cannot change the dashboard without me, it has too many."**
