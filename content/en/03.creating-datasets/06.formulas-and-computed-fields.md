# Formulas and Computed Fields

Two questions decide where a calculation lives: **is it per row or after aggregation**, and **does it belong to the company or to this screen**.

## Per row, or after aggregation

A formula node computes **per row**, before any grouping. That is correct for anything derived arithmetically from values on the same row:

- `NetAmount = ListAmount - DiscountAmount`
- `DaysToClose = CloseDate - CreatedDate`
- `IsEnterprise = Segment == "Enterprise"`

It is wrong for anything that needs a total first. And the most common wrong answer in analytics lives exactly here:

::warning{icon="i-lucide-divide"}
**The average of ratios is not the ratio of totals.** Namilio's quotes carry a discount per deal. Averaging those per-deal rates weights a $5,000 deal exactly the same as a $2M one, so a handful of heavily discounted small deals drags the "average discount" somewhere the company never actually discounted. The number people want is total discount divided by total list value — computed *after* aggregation, never as an average of a per-row column.
::

The same trap wears other clothes: average conversion rate across reps, average win rate across segments, average utilisation across accounts. Each one silently gives every unit equal weight, when the business almost always means weighted by size.

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Ask what the denominator is"
    description: "If the metric is a rate, name its numerator and denominator explicitly. 'Discount rate' is ambiguous; 'total discount ÷ total list value' is not, and writing it that way makes the aggregation order obvious."
  - title: "Aggregate both parts, then divide"
    description: "Sum the numerator, sum the denominator, divide the totals. In a recipe that is an aggregate node followed by a formula on the aggregated output."
  - title: "Only average a rate deliberately"
    description: "An unweighted average of rates is occasionally what you want — 'the typical rep's win rate' genuinely treats reps as equal units. When you mean that, say so in the metric name, because everybody else will assume weighted."

**This is the mechanical half of a metric contract.** Section 7 argues the organisational half — how two teams agree on a definition. Computing it once in a recipe is how that agreement is *enforced* rather than merely documented, because a definition that lives in eleven widgets is a definition that will be eleven definitions within a year.

## Bucketing

A transform node buckets a continuous value into named bands: deal size into Small / Mid / Large, utilisation into Under-consuming / Healthy / In overage, tenure into cohorts.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-ruler"}
**Bucket once, in the recipe, and name the bands in the dataset.** If each dashboard buckets its own way, a "Large" deal means one thing on the sales dashboard and another on the finance one, and the disagreement surfaces in a meeting rather than in a review. Band boundaries are a business decision worth writing down beside the metric definitions — including the awkward ones, like whether utilisation of exactly 55% is at risk or healthy.
::

## Nulls, and why they matter more than they look

A null is not zero. It is "we do not know", and the two behave differently in every aggregation:

- **Sum** ignores nulls. A sum over a column that is 40% null is a sum over 60% of the data, reported without comment.
- **Average** ignores nulls in the denominator too, so the average is of the rows that had a value — which is usually what you want and rarely what you checked.
- **Count** distinguishes count of rows from count of non-null values, and confusing them is a common quiet error.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-circle-slash"}
**Namilio's CSAT surveys are the worked example.** Only about 42% of closed cases have a survey. Average CSAT therefore describes the people who *chose to respond*, not the customers — and respondents skew to the delighted and the furious. The number is not wrong; it answers a narrower question than its label claims. Section 10 builds it with the response rate displayed beside it permanently, because a satisfaction score without a response rate is an advertisement rather than a measurement.
::
