interview:
  - q: "How do you know when a dashboard is finished?"
    a: "When the person it is for can make the decision it exists for, quickly, without me. Not when every requested widget is present. I start by writing the decision down — who makes it, how often, and what changes as a result — and anything that does not serve that sentence does not go on the screen. If I cannot write the sentence, I am arranging charts rather than designing a dashboard."
  - q: "A stakeholder wants a ninth widget. What do you say?"
    a: "I ask what they would do differently depending on what it showed. Asked kindly that distinguishes a widget they need from one they are curious about, and curiosity is legitimate — it belongs in a lens rather than on the screen six people check every Monday. If there is a real decision behind it, something else usually comes off, because attention is the scarce resource, not space."
  - q: "What is faceting and when would you turn it off?"
    a: "Widgets built on the same dataset filter each other by default, which is what makes a dashboard a system rather than a page of pictures — most people will never build a lens but everyone clicks a bar. I turn it off for a reference widget: a company total or a quarter target that must stay fixed while everything else filters, because if it narrows with the selection the comparison it exists for disappears exactly when it becomes useful. Also for widgets at a different grain — Namilio's platform health has no account key, so faceting it by an account selection would imply a relationship the data cannot support."
  - q: "How do you use colour?"
    a: "Sparingly, and never alone. Thresholds come from the business — the number at which somebody does something differently — not from splitting a range into thirds. Every coloured delta also carries an arrow or a label, because red-green is the common colour vision deficiency and colour does not survive a greyscale screenshot. And I set the direction of goodness per metric: churn falling is good, and a default palette will paint it red."
  - q: "A dashboard takes twelve seconds to load. Where do you start?"
    a: "Count the distinct queries rather than the widgets. A screen with twelve widgets that could share four queries is usually the whole problem, and consolidating changes nothing about how it looks. Then whether it is aggregating a very fine grain at query time, which is a case for pre-aggregating in the recipe if many people open it daily. Then wide tables, which are slow and unreadable at the same time. And I measure as a normal user, because my admin account may skip security evaluation that a real viewer pays for on every query."
  - q: "What is the risk of heavy binding use?"
    a: "It is logic stored in widget configuration — it cannot be read at a glance, does not diff, and is not documented anywhere the next person looks. A dashboard with twenty bindings works beautifully and becomes untouchable, so the person who inherits it builds a new one beside it, and that is how an org ends up with four pipeline dashboards and an argument about which is right. I use a handful, favour dynamic titles, and write down anything not obvious from the screen."
  - q: "Chart or table?"
    a: "Chart for shape, table for identity. If the question is 'which specific accounts', a table is the correct widget rather than a fallback — ten account names in a bar chart are unreadable and the names are the point. What I avoid is a table of every row with a scrollbar, which has stopped being a dashboard widget and become a data export; if people genuinely need the full list, that is a download."
  - q: "What is the last thing you do before sharing a dashboard?"
    a: "Two checks. Reconcile the headline against something independently true — the dataset's stated totals, a finance figure, a report people already trust. And open it as a restricted user rather than as myself, because an administrator sees everything and every security mistake is invisible to exactly the person who made it."

# Designing Dashboards: Interview Questions

Design questions are where candidates most often reach for chart types. The strong answers are about scope and refusal.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The answer that lands best** is the one about the ninth widget. "I ask what they would do differently depending on what it showed" demonstrates that you have had the conversation, that you can say no without being difficult, and that you understand a dashboard's cost is attention rather than build time. Almost nobody says it.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"A dashboard that supports no decision is decoration."**
2. **"Colour plus one other channel, always — an arrow or a label."**
3. **"Count queries, not widgets, when it is slow."**
