# Explorer Basics

Four controls. Everything else in exploration is a combination of them.

## Measures answer "how much"

The measure is what gets aggregated: sum of Amount, count of rows, average of DaysToClose, max of CloseDate. Changing the measure changes the question without changing the chart's shape at all — which is worth noticing, because it is the cleanest demonstration that a chart is a rendering of a query rather than a thing in itself.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-hash"}
**Count of rows means count of rows, not count of the thing you have in mind.** At Namilio's line-item grain, 1,378 rows is 677 opportunities. If your dataset's grain is finer than the entity you are counting, use a distinct count on that entity's key. This is the same grain question from section 3 wearing different clothes, and it catches people who thought they had learned it.
::

## Groupings answer "by what"

Add a grouping and one bar becomes many. This is where the analysis is: by segment, by region, by month, by rep, by source.

::note{icon="i-lucide-layers"}
**The second grouping is where most charts die.** One grouping is a comparison. Two is a comparison of comparisons, and the reader has to hold both in their head while decoding a legend. Occasionally that is exactly the point — revenue by segment over time genuinely needs both. Far more often the second grouping was added because it was possible. When you add one, ask what question it answers that the first did not.
::

## Filters answer "among which"

Filters narrow the rows. Two points worth internalising early:

- **A null is not excluded by an inequality.** `Stage != "Closed Lost"` also removes every row where Stage is null, which is rarely intended and never announced.
- **A filter on the exploration is not a filter on the dashboard.** Section 5 covers how dashboard filters, faceting and this interact; for now, know that a lens filter belongs to the lens.

## Sorting is a default worth changing immediately

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Sorted by the grouping"
    subtitle: "The default"
    icon: "i-lucide-arrow-down-a-z"
    pros:
      - "Stable ordering — a segment stays where it was last week"
      - "Right when the categories have a natural order, like months"
    cons:
      - "Alphabetical order hides the ranking, which is usually the whole point"
      - "Readers assume a bar chart is ranked and misread it when it is not"
    verdict: "Keep for time. Change for categories."
  - title: "Sorted by the measure"
    subtitle: "Almost always what you want"
    icon: "i-lucide-arrow-down-wide-narrow"
    pros:
      - "The biggest thing reads first, which is how people read charts"
      - "Makes the comparison immediate rather than a decoding exercise"
    cons:
      - "Order shifts between refreshes, which can surprise a regular viewer"
    verdict: "Default to this for any categorical grouping."
---
::

## Limits, and the honesty problem

Showing the top ten of 140 accounts is good design. Showing the top ten of 140 accounts *labelled as if it were all of them* is misleading.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-tag"}
**Put the limit in the title.** "Top 10 accounts by ARR" rather than "Accounts by ARR". It costs four words and it removes an entire category of misreading — including the one where somebody sums the visible bars and concludes the total is wrong.
::
