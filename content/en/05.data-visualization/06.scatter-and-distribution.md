# Scatter and Distribution

Two chart families that answer questions the rest of this section cannot — and that most dashboards skip in favour of an average that hides the finding.

## Scatter: do these move together?

One point per entity, one measure on each axis. That is the whole idea, and it is the only common chart that shows a *relationship between two measures* rather than a measure across a dimension.

::lesson-cards
---
columns: 3
items:
  - title: "The shape is the finding"
    icon: "i-lucide-trending-up"
    description: "A cloud sloping up means the two rise together. A shapeless cloud means they do not — which is a real and useful result, and one no KPI tile can deliver."
  - title: "The outliers are named"
    icon: "i-lucide-locate"
    description: "The points far from the cloud are the interesting accounts. Label them, or make the chart clickable so the reader can find out who they are."
  - title: "Clusters are segments"
    icon: "i-lucide-group"
    description: "Two separate clouds usually mean two populations behaving differently — which is a sign you should be splitting the metric by that dimension everywhere else too."
---
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**A slope is not causation, and everybody knows that and forgets it anyway.** Namilio's accounts with more support cases also have more ARR — because large accounts have more of everything. The honest version normalises: cases per $100k of ARR. Any scatter of two raw volumes across entities of wildly different size will show a slope that is really just size.
::

**Bubble** adds a third measure as the *area* of each point. It works for one more dimension and no further — four encodings on one chart (x, y, area, colour) is a puzzle rather than a chart.

## Distribution: what does the spread look like

This is the family most often replaced by an average, and the replacement is usually where the insight goes.

::note{icon="i-lucide-bar-chart-4"}
**Namilio's company utilisation is 78%.** That single number is the average of accounts at 30% — under-consuming, at genuine churn risk — and accounts at 130%, in overage and heading for a renegotiation. Both need action, in opposite directions, and the average describes neither. Charting the distribution in bands shows two populations immediately, and the dashboard stops being a summary and becomes a work list.
::

CRM Analytics has no dedicated histogram widget, so the practical approach is to bucket in the recipe and chart the buckets:

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Bucket in the recipe, not the widget"
    description: "Bands defined once — under 55, 55-85, 85-105, over 105 — so every dashboard means the same thing by 'at risk'. This is the bucketing lesson from the data preparation section, used in anger."
  - title: "Chart the buckets as a column chart"
    description: "Count of accounts per band, bands in order along the axis. It reads as a histogram and it is built from a grouping."
  - title: "Put the count on each band"
    description: "The shape tells you there are two populations; the labels tell you how many accounts are in the one you have to call."
  - title: "Keep the average beside it, not instead of it"
    description: "The average is still useful as a single tracked number over time. It belongs next to the distribution, not in place of it."
---
::

## When to reach for each

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Reach for a distribution"
    subtitle: "Before reporting any average"
    icon: "i-lucide-chart-column"
    pros:
      - "Reveals two populations hiding in one number"
      - "Makes thresholds visible and arguable"
      - "Turns a summary into a list of accounts to act on"
    cons:
      - "Takes more space than a KPI tile"
    verdict: "Ask 'is this bimodal?' before publishing a mean."
  - title: "Reach for a scatter"
    subtitle: "Before claiming a relationship"
    icon: "i-lucide-scatter-chart"
    pros:
      - "Shows whether a claimed relationship exists at all"
      - "Names the exceptions"
      - "Settles arguments that two bar charts cannot"
    cons:
      - "Needs normalising when entities differ wildly in size"
      - "Less familiar to some executive audiences"
    verdict: "The most under-used chart in go-to-market analytics."
