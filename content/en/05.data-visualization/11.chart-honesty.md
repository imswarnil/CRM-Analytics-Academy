# Chart Honesty

Every number correct, and the conclusion wrong. This lesson is about the handful of formatting decisions that do that, and the checklist that catches them.

::note{icon="i-lucide-eye"}
**The test is not "is this accurate", it is "what will somebody conclude in four seconds, and is that true?"** Accuracy is the easy part and it is nearly always fine. The conclusion is where dashboards fail, and the failures are systematic enough to be checked.
::

## The six that account for most of it

::field-table
---
dataset: honesty failures
grain: one row per failure
fields:
  - name: "The missing denominator"
    type: "most common by far"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Counts where a rate was the question. 'EMEA has the most cases' means little without cases per account or per $100k of ARR — the biggest region will have the most of everything. Ask what the denominator is before charting any total."
  - name: "A truncated bar axis"
    type: "misstates ratios"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Bar length is the encoding. Starting at 40 turns a 20% gap into a 3x visual gap. Legitimate on a line, where slope is the encoding, and only with the range labelled."
  - name: "An average hiding a distribution"
    type: "hides the finding"
    role: "measure"
    note: "88% utilisation across accounts at 30% and 140%. If the shape is bimodal, the average describes nobody."
  - name: "A dual axis"
    type: "invents relationships"
    role: "key"
    note: "The crossing point is a formatting choice that reads as an event. Two charts sharing an x-axis say the same thing without the implication."
  - name: "An unlabelled limit"
    type: "misstates the whole"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "'Accounts by ARR' showing ten of 140. Readers sum the bars and conclude the total is wrong. Four words in the title fixes it permanently."
  - name: "Colour doing the work alone"
    type: "excludes readers"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Red-green is the common deficiency, and colour dies in greyscale, in a projector and in a printed board pack. Colour plus an arrow, a sign or a label — always."
---
::

## Aggregation is a claim too

Sum, average, median and distinct count of the same column answer four different questions, and the widget title usually says none of them.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-sigma"}
**"Average deal size" is ambiguous and the two readings differ a lot.** Mean is pulled upward by a handful of enterprise deals; median describes the typical deal. The Academy has both a $36,000 flat Enterprise Programme fee and hundreds of $240 Academy Pro subscriptions, so the mean and the median tell genuinely different stories — and both are correct. Put the function in the label: "median deal size", not "average".
::

## Time is where comparisons go wrong

- **Which date?** Created, closed, last modified, stage entry. The same data grouped by a different date tells a different story, and the chart cannot show which you picked.
- **Which comparison?** Up 12% against last month, the same month last year, or plan — three answers that frequently disagree in sign.
- **Partial periods.** The current month is always down, because it is not finished. Either exclude it, shade it, or label it — a dashboard that shows an incomplete month beside complete ones manufactures a decline every single month.

## The review checklist

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Read the title as a sentence"
    description: "Does it name the measure, the period, the filter and any limit? If somebody screenshots this widget into a message with no context, does it still say what it is?"
  - title: "Check the axis start and the scale"
    description: "Zero for bars. Labelled range for lines. Log scales announced as log."
  - title: "Ask whether a rate was needed"
    description: "Any total compared across entities of different size probably needed a denominator."
  - title: "Look for a hidden distribution"
    description: "Before publishing any average, ask whether the spread is the finding."
  - title: "Turn it greyscale in your head"
    description: "If the message disappears without colour, the message was carried by colour alone."
  - title: "Ask someone what it says"
    description: "Ten seconds of a colleague who has not seen it. If their four-second conclusion is not the one you intended, the chart is wrong even though every number is right."
---
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-shield-check"}
**None of this is about being cautious to the point of uselessness.** A chart that refuses to make a point is its own kind of failure. The goal is that the point it makes is the one the data supports — which usually means one more sentence in the title and one fewer clever encoding.
::
