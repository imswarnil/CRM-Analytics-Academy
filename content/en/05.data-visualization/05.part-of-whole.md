# Part-of-Whole Charts

Composition is the most over-charted comparison in analytics. Almost every donut in a real dashboard would be better as a table or a sorted bar.

## Why angles are hard

People compare **lengths** well and **angles** badly. A bar twice as long is obviously twice as long; a slice of 23% against one of 19% is a coin flip by eye. That single fact governs the whole family.

::note{icon="i-lucide-pie-chart"}
**A donut works with two or three slices**, where the shape itself carries a message — "about a third of revenue comes from one product". Past four, the reader is reading labels, and once they are reading labels a table does the job better and takes less space.
::

## The family, honestly

grain: one row per type

    type: "2-4 slices"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Good when the shape is the message and the parts are few. The donut's hole is useful: put the total in it, which fixes the biggest weakness — that a share chart hides absolute size."
  - name: "Stacked bar"
    type: "2-4 segments, compared across groups"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Total height compares groups; segments give a rough mix. Only the bottom segment sits on a shared baseline, so the others cannot be compared segment-to-segment across bars."
  - name: "100% stacked"
    type: "mix over time"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Every bar the same height, so shifts in mix are obvious and size is invisible. Genuinely useful for 'is the channel mix changing', and it must be paired with a total somewhere or the reader concludes nothing grew."
  - name: "Treemap"
    type: "many parts, hierarchy"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Area-encoded, so it handles twenty parts where a donut handles four. Comparing two similar rectangles is still hard, and comparing non-adjacent ones is harder. Best as an overview that invites a click."
  - name: "Sorted table with a share column"
    type: "when values matter"
    role: "key"
    note: "Unfashionable, and correct more often than the rest combined. Exact values, sortable, readable at any count, and it survives being pasted into a document."
---
::

## The test

::tip{icon="i-lucide-help-circle"}
**Ask what the reader does with the answer.** If the action depends on knowing the rank and rough proportion — "most of our revenue is Enterprise" — a chart is right. If it depends on the number itself — "Enterprise is 43.2% and the target is 45%" — they need the number, and a chart makes them estimate something you could have told them.
::

## Composition over time

Two distinct questions, often confused:

- **"Did the mix change?"** → 100% stacked, or a set of lines of each share. Size deliberately hidden.
- **"Did each part grow?"** → separate lines, or small multiples. Mix deliberately hidden.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-layers"}
**A stacked area over time tries to answer both and reliably answers neither.** Every band above the bottom moves because of what is beneath it, so a segment can look like it is growing while it is flat — its baseline rose. If somebody needs to know whether Mid-Market grew, give Mid-Market its own line.
::

## Namilio's worked example

Revenue by product across six products, three pricing models. A donut of six slices communicates almost nothing; a sorted bar with the value on each bar answers "which products matter" instantly; and the honest composition question — "is the usage business becoming a bigger share of revenue?" — is a 100% stacked column by month with total ARR on a KPI tile beside it.
