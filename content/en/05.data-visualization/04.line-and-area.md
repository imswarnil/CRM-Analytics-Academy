# Line and Area Charts

The chart for change over time. Its failures are quieter than a bar chart's, which makes them more durable.

## Lines are for continuous change

A line implies that the values between the points are meaningful — that the measure existed continuously and you sampled it. That is true of ARR, utilisation, error rate and pipeline value. It is not true of "deals closed per month", which is a count of discrete events.

::note{icon="i-lucide-spline"}
**Use columns for counted events and a line for a level.** Deals closed in March is a count that exists only at month granularity; drawing it as a line implies there was a moment mid-March when 7.5 deals had closed. Nobody is genuinely confused by this, but the column version reads faster because it matches what the number is.
::

## How many series

Four is about the limit, and that assumes they are separable. Past that, readers spend their attention on the legend rather than on the trend.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-highlighter"}
**When many series genuinely exist, grey them and highlight one.** All segments in a light neutral, the one being discussed in the accent. The reader gets the context and the point, and the chart survives being screenshotted into a document.
::

## Gaps mean something

A month with no rows is ambiguous: was the value zero, or is it unknown?

::warning{icon="i-lucide-unlink"}
**Joining straight across a gap asserts that the measure moved smoothly through it.** For a count that was genuinely zero, plot zero. For a period where data is missing — a sync that failed, a product that did not exist yet — break the line. A continuous line across a month when the pipeline was down is a claim the data cannot support, and it is the most common way a chart lies without any number being wrong.
::

The Academy's data has exactly this: `IMPL`, Implementation Services, launched in May 2025, so April has no rows for it. A line drawn from the axis through April implies a service that existed and sold nothing. A break says it did not exist.

## The baseline question

For lines, unlike bars, a non-zero baseline is often legitimate. The encoding is the *slope*, not the distance from the axis, and forcing zero on a metric that lives between 94% and 112% flattens every movement that matters.

## The dual axis

Two measures, two scales, one chart. It is the single most misleading widget available.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-x"}
**The point where the two lines cross is a formatting decision.** Move either axis and the crossing moves with it, but readers interpret a crossing as an event — "revenue overtook cost in June". Nothing overtook anything; the two measures are in different units and cannot be compared at all. If the relationship is the question, use a scatter plot; if both trends matter, use two stacked charts sharing an x-axis, where the reader can see each shape without being invited to compare heights.
::

The one defensible dual axis is the same measure in two units — revenue and revenue-as-percentage-of-plan — where the axes are genuinely locked to each other.

## Area charts

An area is a line with the region beneath it filled. The fill claims the *quantity under the curve* is meaningful — cumulative volume, total consumption.

- **One series, meaningful accumulation:** legitimate and readable.
- **Stacked areas of related parts summing to a total:** legitimate, with the same caveat as stacked bars — only the bottom band has a stable baseline.
- **Several unrelated series overlaid with transparency:** decoration. Use lines.
