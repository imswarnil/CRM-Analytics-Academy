interview:
  - q: "How do you choose a chart type?"
    a: "From the comparison the reader has to make, not from the shape of the data. There are six: rank, trend, composition, distribution, relationship, and one value against a target. Naming which one I am serving usually eliminates all but two candidates. 'I have a dimension and a measure so I'll use a bar' is how most charts get chosen and it is why dashboards end up as walls of bars."
  - q: "When is a table better than a chart?"
    a: "When the identity of each row matters or the exact values do. 'Which ten accounts are at risk' needs the name, the ARR, the utilisation, the trend and the owner — five attributes, where a chart encodes two. The table is the correct widget there, not a fallback. What I avoid is a table with a scrollbar, which has stopped being a dashboard widget and become an export."
  - q: "What is wrong with a dual-axis chart?"
    a: "The point where the lines cross is decided by where you set the two axes, and readers interpret a crossing as an event. The two measures are in different units and cannot be compared at all. If the relationship is the question I use a scatter plot; if both trends matter, two charts sharing an x-axis. The one defensible dual axis is the same measure in two units, where the axes are locked together."
  - q: "When may you truncate the y-axis?"
    a: "On a line, where the encoding is slope and forcing zero on something that lives between 94 and 112 percent hides everything that matters — with the range labelled. Never on a bar, because bar length is the encoding, so truncating misstates the ratio between bars while every label stays accurate. The dishonesty is not the truncation; it is the truncation the reader did not notice."
  - q: "A stakeholder wants a pie chart of nine product lines. What do you say?"
    a: "That people compare angles badly past about four slices, so nine slices will make them read labels — and once they are reading labels, a sorted bar or a table with a share column does the job better in less space. Then I ask what they do with the answer: if it is 'which are biggest', a bar; if it is the exact percentages, a table. I try to come back with the thing they actually wanted rather than a lecture about pie charts."
  - q: "What is the most common dishonest chart in business reporting?"
    a: "A raw total that needed a denominator. 'EMEA has the most support cases' is nearly always just 'EMEA is the biggest region' — the fix is cases per account or per hundred thousand of ARR. It is more common than truncated axes and it is harder to spot, because nothing about the chart looks manipulated."
  - q: "How do you handle an average that hides a bimodal distribution?"
    a: "Chart the distribution beside it. Our utilisation averages 88%, which is the mean of accounts at 30% — under-consuming and at churn risk — and accounts at 140%, in overage and heading for a renegotiation. Both need action in opposite directions and the average shows neither. I bucket in the recipe so the bands mean the same thing everywhere, chart the counts per band, and keep the average as a tracked number rather than as the answer."
  - q: "How do you review your own chart?"
    a: "I ask what somebody would conclude in four seconds and whether that is true — accuracy is the easy part and it is nearly always fine. Then a short checklist: does the title carry the measure, period, filter and any limit; does the axis start where it should; did a total need a rate; is there a distribution hiding behind an average; does the message survive greyscale. Then I ask a colleague who has not seen it what it says. If their conclusion is not mine, the chart is wrong even though every number on it is right."

# Data Visualization: Interview Questions

Chart questions separate candidates with taste from candidates with a method. Taste produces confident opinions about pie charts. A method produces a reason.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The answer that lands best** is the denominator one. Almost everybody can recite that truncated axes are bad; very few volunteer that the most common dishonest chart is a raw count that needed a rate. It shows you have reviewed real dashboards rather than read a list of chart crimes.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"Pick the chart from the comparison, not from the data type."**
2. **"Zero baseline for bars, because length is the encoding; labelled range for lines, because slope is."**
3. **"Before publishing an average, check whether the distribution is the finding."**
