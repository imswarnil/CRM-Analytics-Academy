# Bar and Column Charts

Same chart, two orientations, and more dashboards are decided by its sort order than by anything else in this section.

## Horizontal or vertical

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Horizontal bars"
    subtitle: "Categories that are words"
    icon: "i-lucide-align-left"
    pros:
      - "Labels read naturally, at any length"
      - "Ranking reads top to bottom, matching how people scan"
      - "Handles many categories without rotating text"
    cons:
      - "Feels unfamiliar to people expecting columns"
    verdict: "The default for segments, products, reps, sources."
  - title: "Vertical columns"
    subtitle: "Categories that are periods"
    icon: "i-lucide-align-end-horizontal"
    pros:
      - "Time reads left to right, which readers expect"
      - "Good for a small number of short labels"
      - "Pairs naturally with a line on the same axis"
    cons:
      - "Long labels rotate, and rotated labels slow reading"
    verdict: "Months, quarters, stages — anything with a natural order."
---
::

## Sorting is the setting that matters

CRM Analytics sorts by the **grouping** by default, which means alphabetical for text categories. That is almost never what a bar chart is for.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-arrow-down-wide-narrow"}
**Readers assume bars are ranked.** Presented with a bar chart, the eye takes the first bar as the biggest and checks no further. When the order is alphabetical, that assumption is silently wrong and the misreading is invisible — the chart is correct, the reader is not. Sort descending by the measure for any categorical grouping, and keep the grouping's own order only for time and for genuinely ordered stages.
::

## Grouped or stacked

The question is which comparison you are protecting:

::field-table
---
dataset: grouped vs stacked
grain: one row per case
fields:
  - name: "Compare sub-categories across groups"
    type: "grouped"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Every sub-bar sits on the same baseline, so lengths are comparable by eye. In a stack, only the bottom segment has a shared baseline — the ones above it start at different heights and cannot be compared honestly."
  - name: "Compare totals across groups"
    type: "stacked"
    role: "measure"
    note: "The stack's full height is the total, and the segments give a rough sense of mix. This is the legitimate use and it is genuinely good at it."
  - name: "Show mix as a percentage"
    type: "100% stacked"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Every bar the same height, segments as share. Excellent for 'has the mix shifted', and it destroys any sense of absolute size — so pair it with a total somewhere."
  - name: "More than four segments"
    type: "neither"
    role: "key"
    note: "Five-plus segments is a table with a share column, or a sorted bar of the top few with the rest as 'Other'. A stack of nine colours is a legend-reading exercise."
---
::

## The baseline rule

For bars, **the length is the encoding**. A bar twice as long means twice the value, and readers rely on that without thinking.

::note{icon="i-lucide-ruler"}
**So a bar chart's axis must start at zero.** Truncating it to 40 because everything sits between 40 and 60 turns a 20% difference into a bar three times longer, and the chart lies about the ratio while every label remains accurate. If small differences matter and zero makes them invisible, that is a signal to use a line chart, a dot plot or a delta-against-target — not to move the baseline of a bar.
::

## Practical settings worth changing

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Sort descending by the measure"
    description: "For every categorical grouping. This is the single highest-value change on the widget."
  - title: "Limit to the top N, and say so in the title"
    description: "Ten bars is readable; forty is a texture. 'Top 10 accounts by ARR' rather than 'Accounts by ARR' — otherwise someone sums the visible bars and reports the total as wrong."
  - title: "Put the value on the bar"
    description: "Data labels remove the axis-reading step entirely for a small number of bars. Drop them once the chart is dense enough that they collide."
  - title: "Use one colour"
    description: "A separate colour per category encodes nothing — the category is already the axis label. Spend colour on the one bar that needs attention, and leave the rest neutral."
---
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-palette"}
**One colour, one exception.** A bar chart where every bar is a different colour looks designed and communicates nothing extra. A bar chart in a single neutral, with the one bar above or below threshold in the accent, communicates a finding before the reader has read a label.
::
