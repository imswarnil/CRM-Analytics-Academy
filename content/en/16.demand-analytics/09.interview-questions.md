description: "Rehearse the demand-side interview: measuring AI search visibility, SEO when rank stops predicting traffic, CAC when some revenue is one-off, attribution model choice, and answering a request that cannot honestly be built."
interview:
  - q: "How would you measure whether AI assistants recommend our product?"
    a: "I'd define a prompt list from the language prospects actually use on sales calls rather than from a keyword tool, cluster those prompts by topic and buyer stage, and run each one several times per engine on a schedule — five runs is enough — because assistant output is non-deterministic and a single check is an anecdote. From that I'd compute Share of Voice as mentions over total runs, and keep two more metrics separate from it: recommendation rate, which is how often we're the actual pick given that we were named, and citation rate, which is how often our own URL is the source. Those three separate different problems with different fixes — not being named is a content gap, being named but never recommended is positioning, and not being cited means our pages aren't structured to be quotable. I'd split everything by engine because citation behaviour varies enormously, and I'd be upfront that there's no industry benchmark, so the value is in the trend and in who gets named beside us."
  - q: "Our #3 ranking keyword lost 40% of its clicks and the rank didn't change. What happened?"
    a: "Almost certainly the result page changed around us — most likely an AI Overview now answers the query above the links, or a competitor gained a rich result that pushed us below the fold. The diagnostic is to plot average position and clicks on the same timeline: if position is flat and clicks fall, the ranking is fine and the click has been taken, which is not something you fix by optimising harder. I'd check the AI Overview exposure flag by intent, because informational queries get hit far harder than commercial ones. Then the response splits: for commercial and transactional clusters it's worth restructuring the page to be the thing an answer has to cite, and for purely informational clusters the honest conclusion is sometimes that the click is gone permanently and the content should be measured by assistant visibility instead of sessions. That's an uncomfortable recommendation and it's usually the right one."
  - q: "What's wrong with blended CAC?"
    a: "It averages things that shouldn't be averaged. At a company with a self-serve tier, a large share of new customers arrive at close to zero acquisition cost, which drags the blended figure far below what it actually costs to win a mid-market or enterprise account — so the number is true and useless for any decision. I'd publish it because boards ask for it, label it reference only, and put segment CAC beside it as the version you'd actually act on. I'd also check the paid CAC arithmetic carefully, because the most common error I see is dividing total marketing spend by only the paid-sourced customers, which mixes the denominators and understates CAC substantially. And I'd pair every CAC with payback months computed on gross profit rather than revenue — with margins ranging from about 35% to 88% by offering, using revenue makes payback look roughly twice as good as it is."
  - q: "Which attribution model would you use?"
    a: "I'd build first touch and last touch side by side and treat the gap between them as the output, because the gap is where the actual decision lives. First touch credits demand creation and favours docs, content and organic; last touch credits conversion and systematically over-credits brand and paid search, since someone searching your company name was already sold. With a long enterprise cycle, running last touch alone will quietly defund the top of the funnel. I'd avoid multi-touch unless the touch data genuinely exists — with two stored columns, a weighted model is invented precision and the weights become the argument instead of the insight. Alongside both I'd show influenced pipeline, which is just 'of deals that closed, how many had any touch from this channel', percentages deliberately summing over 100%, because it answers 'would we miss this channel' without pretending to divide credit. And whatever I ship, the model name goes in the chart title."
  - q: "Marketing wants revenue attributed to individual keywords. How do you handle it?"
    a: "I'd say plainly that I can't build it honestly and explain why in one sentence — the session that read the page was anonymous, there's no identifier tying it to the account, and the deal closed months later after several other touches. Then I'd name what I think the real question is, usually whether content earns its budget, and offer three things I can actually build: which keyword clusters precede a signup by landing page, first-touch revenue for organic as a whole with the model labelled, and influenced pipeline wherever organic appears at all. If they still want the single number, I'll ship it with the assumption printed next to it, because then they own the model, which is the right place for it. Refusing outright is the wrong move — there's a real question underneath and the relationship matters more than being technically correct at somebody."
  - q: "Why separate AI assistant referrals into their own channel?"
    a: "Because default channel groupings put them in Direct or Referral, and at a product-led company that buries the best-converting traffic you have. In our data assistant referrals convert to signup at around 4.9% against about 2.8% for organic, and they arrive with higher intent because they've effectively been recommended. If that's hidden inside Direct, nobody can make a case for investing in the work that produces it. Mechanically it's a formula field in the recipe matching the known assistant hosts, done once so every dashboard agrees. Two caveats I'd state on the dashboard: some assistants strip the referrer so the number is a floor rather than a total, and because the channel is growing from a small base it needs its own trend line — on a stacked chart it's invisible and gets ignored for another year."
  - q: "How do you decide whether to cut a channel with a high cost per lead?"
    a: "I wouldn't decide on cost per lead at all, because it ranks channels by volume efficiency and says nothing about what closes. The example I'd give is conferences: worst cost per lead by a wide margin, and a disproportionate share of strategic pipeline. Cut them on cost per lead and you lose the seven-figure deals. So I'd plot cost per MQL against lead-to-opportunity rate to get four quadrants — cheap and good means scale, cheap and bad is the trap that lead-volume dashboards reward, expensive and good is worth keeping, expensive and bad is the cut. I'd add win rate beside pipeline ROI, because a channel can generate abundant cheap pipeline that never closes. And I'd set a minimum volume floor before ranking anything, or the top of the efficiency chart is a campaign that got two lucky leads and somebody tries to triple its budget."
  - q: "Our APAC region has 20% of sessions and 13% of closed-won revenue. Is that a problem?"
    a: "It's a question, not a finding, and there are four explanations with very different price tags. Coverage: demand arrives and nobody follows up, which I'd check with speed-to-lead and SQLs per active AE in region. Fit: the traffic is students learning for free, which shows up as disqualification reasons clustering on 'free course only' — headcount will not fix that. I'd also check the classroom data, because APAC carries 45% of classroom revenue, and a region that buys in person may simply not be buying online. Commercial friction: currency, payment methods, local entity, data residency, which shows up as losses on failed vendor review. And timing: if half that pipeline was created inside one sales-cycle length, the region should look bad because its revenue hasn't happened yet. That last one is the trap — comparing a young region's revenue to a mature one's is comparing a partial year to a full one, so I'd filter to deals created more than one cycle ago before anyone draws a conclusion, and put that filter on the face of the dashboard."
  - q: "How do you compute LTV for a prepaid pool, like classroom seat-days?"
    a: "Carefully, and I'd show two numbers. The textbook formula divides ARPA times gross margin by a churn rate, and it assumes revenue per account is stable and that churn is an event — neither is true for a prepaid pool, where an account renews for whatever it actually used and can shrink 40% without ever cancelling. So I'd publish that figure for the seat plans where it's genuinely valid, and label it an estimate elsewhere. The honest version is cohort-realised value: group accounts by signup month, sum each cohort's revenue indexed by months since signup, and plot cumulative revenue per cohort. Lines that keep climbing mean expansion is outrunning contraction, which is the single most important fact about a business that sells into teams and completely invisible in a churn rate. The caveat to state is truncation — a three-month-old cohort has three months of revenue against a full CAC, so recent cohorts always look bad and should be greyed out rather than compared."

# Demand Analytics: Interview Questions

Nine questions on modules 8's material. Almost all of them are really testing one thing: **will you publish a number you cannot defend?**

::note{icon="i-lucide-shield"}
The demand side is where analytics credibility is won or lost, because it is where the honest answer is most often "that cannot be measured, here is what can". Candidates who volunteer the caveat before being pushed on it interview very well.
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-list-checks"}
**Three habits that show up in every strong answer above.** Name the model or the definition in the chart title. Separate two failures that look like one metric. Put the assumption on the chart rather than in a footnote.
::
