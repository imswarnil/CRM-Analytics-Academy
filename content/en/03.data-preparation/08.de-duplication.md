# De-duplication

"We have duplicates" describes three unrelated problems. Diagnosing which one you have is the whole lesson, because the fix for each is different and two of them should not involve deleting anything.

## The three kinds

::field-table
---
dataset: what "duplicate" means
grain: one row per kind
fields:
  - name: "A join artefact"
    type: "most common, not a duplicate at all"
    role: "key"
    note: "Rows multiplied by a one-to-many join. The records are fine; the query is wrong. The fix is to aggregate the many side before joining, or not to carry the parent measure down — never a de-duplication step, which would delete real data to work around a bad join."
  - name: "Legitimate repetition"
    type: "the intended grain"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "An account appearing eighteen times in a monthly snapshot, a product appearing on many opportunities. This is the grain working correctly. Remove it and you destroy the dataset's purpose."
  - name: "Genuine duplicate records"
    type: "the real problem"
    role: "measure"
    note: "The same real-world thing entered twice — two account records for one company, a lead submitted three times from three forms. This is the only case that calls for de-duplication, and it is the rarest of the three."
---
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-triangle-alert"}
**Deleting rows to fix a fan-out is the most damaging response available.** The total comes down, so it looks like it worked, and you have now removed real records while leaving the wrong join in place — so the next measure added to the same recipe is wrong again, in a different direction, and the dataset no longer matches its source. Diagnose before deleting: count rows before and after each join node and find where they multiplied.
::

## Finding genuine duplicates

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Define what 'the same' means"
    description: "Same email? Same company name and domain? Same name and postcode? This is a business decision and it decides everything downstream — two subsidiaries of one group may or may not be the same account, and the answer differs by company."
  - title: "Count the candidates first"
    description: "Group by the key and count rows per group before touching anything. Thirty duplicate groups is a cleanup; three thousand is a source-system or integration problem, and fixing it downstream is treating a symptom forever."
  - title: "Inspect a sample by eye"
    description: "Pull ten duplicate groups and look at them. Very often they are not duplicates at all — a parent and a subsidiary, two genuinely distinct contacts with a shared inbox — and your matching rule is too loose."
  - title: "Choose a survivor by an explicit rule"
    description: "Most recently modified, most complete, from the preferred source system. Written down, applied consistently, and never 'whichever comes first', because that is stable until the data arrives in a different order."
---
::

## Survivorship is where the value goes

::note{icon="i-lucide-merge"}
**Keeping the newest row often loses the most information.** A duplicate created last week may have three fields populated where the older one has twelve. Real de-duplication usually merges — take the most recent value per field where present, falling back to the older record — rather than picking a row and discarding the rest. That is more work in the recipe and it is the difference between cleaning data and losing it.
::

## Fix it upstream where you can

De-duplication in a recipe is a workaround. It runs nightly, forever, and the source system keeps producing duplicates that every other consumer also has to handle.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-arrow-up"}
**Report the duplicate rate back to whoever owns the source.** A weekly count of new duplicate groups by source is a more valuable deliverable than the cleanup itself: it turns an invisible data-quality problem into a number somebody can own. The recipe-level fix keeps your dashboards correct in the meantime, which is exactly what a workaround should do — hold the line while the real fix happens somewhere else.
::

## Check afterwards

After any de-duplication, compare the row count and the key totals against the source. A cleanup that removed 40 rows and changed revenue by 8% did not remove duplicates — it removed distinct records, and the check is the only thing that catches it before a dashboard does.
