# Missing Values

A null means *we do not know*. A zero means *we know, and it is none*. Every aggregation treats them differently, and confusing them is one of the quietest ways to produce a wrong number.

## What each aggregation does

::field-table
---
dataset: nulls in aggregation
grain: one row per function
fields:
  - name: "Sum"
    type: "skips them"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Returns the total of the rows that had a value. Over a column that is 40% empty, that is a total of 60% of the data presented as a total, with nothing to say so."
  - name: "Average"
    type: "skips them in both parts"
    role: "measure"
    note: "The mean of the rows that had a value — usually what you want, rarely what you checked. Materially different from the mean over all rows if you intended blanks to count as zero."
  - name: "Count"
    type: "depends which count"
    role: "key"
    note: "Row count includes blanks; a distinct count of a column does not. On a sparse field the two differ a lot and neither is wrong."
  - name: "Grouping"
    type: "makes a null bucket"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "Honest, and frequently the most interesting bucket on the chart. Label it 'Unknown' rather than hiding it — 'unassigned' is a real category and usually a work item."
---
::

## Filling a gap is a claim

::warning{icon="i-lucide-pencil"}
**Every fill asserts something.** Zero asserts none. The average asserts typical. The previous value asserts nothing changed. Each is defensible in some situations and each is a statement you are making on behalf of the data, so it belongs in the metric definition rather than buried in a formula node. The question to ask is: *if this fill is wrong, what breaks?* Filling a missing amount with zero drags every average down; filling with the mean invents a deal that never existed.
::

## When filling is legitimate

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Fill"
    subtitle: "The gap has a known meaning"
    icon: "i-lucide-check"
    pros:
      - "A month with no rows for a product that existed: zero is the true value"
      - "A carried-forward status in a snapshot, where no change genuinely means unchanged"
      - "A default that the source system would have applied anyway"
    cons:
      - "Needs writing down, or the next person cannot tell filled from real"
    verdict: "Fine, documented, and preferably in a flagged column."
  - title: "Do not fill"
    subtitle: "The gap is genuine ignorance"
    icon: "i-lucide-x"
    pros:
      - "Nulls propagate honestly — the number excludes what you do not know"
    cons:
      - "Charts show gaps, which look like defects and generate questions"
      - "Somebody downstream may fill it anyway, worse"
    verdict: "Leave it null and label the gap on the chart."
---
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-flag"}
**When you do fill, keep a flag column saying you did.** A boolean `AmountWasImputed` costs nothing and means anybody can exclude filled rows from an analysis that should not include them — and can tell, a year later, which numbers were real. Filling without a flag destroys the information that it was ever missing.
::

## Predictive filling

Estimating a missing value from other columns — an amount inferred from segment and product, a close date inferred from stage and age — is occasionally reasonable and always a bigger claim than a constant.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-brain"}
**If you predict a value and then analyse it, you will find the pattern you predicted with.** Impute amount from segment, then report revenue by segment, and the segment differences you see are partly your own imputation looking back at you. Keep imputed values out of any analysis about the thing they were imputed from, and flag them so somebody else can too.
::

## Missingness as signal

Sometimes the blank is the most informative column in the dataset.

::note{icon="i-lucide-lightbulb"}
**At Namilio, `EconomicBuyerIdentified` being blank correlates with losing** — not because the blank causes the loss, but because a rep who is not engaged does not fill fields in. That makes "is it blank" genuinely predictive and completely unactionable: telling a rep to fill the field in does not create an economic buyer. This is one of the most common ways an otherwise good model produces advice that changes nothing, and the Einstein Discovery section returns to it.
::

## The response-rate case

Namilio's CSAT covers roughly 42% of closed cases. The average is arithmetically correct and answers a narrower question than its label claims: it describes people who chose to respond, and response skews to the delighted and the furious.

The fix is not imputation. It is publishing the response rate beside the score, permanently — a satisfaction number without one is an advertisement rather than a measurement.
