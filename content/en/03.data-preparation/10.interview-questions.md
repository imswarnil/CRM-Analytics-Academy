interview:
  - q: "Walk me through how data gets into a dataset."
    a: "A connection defines the source. Data sync pulls selected objects and selected fields into CRM Analytics' own store on a schedule, so recipes read a local copy rather than hitting the system of record on every run. A recipe transforms that — joins, filters, formulas, aggregations — and writes a dataset. The dataset is materialised and columnar, and lenses and dashboards read it. Two clocks, sync and recipes, and the data is as old as the earlier of them."
  - q: "What is the failure that never errors?"
    a: "A recipe scheduled before the sync it depends on has finished. It runs successfully, on yesterday's data, every night, and Monitor shows green throughout. When somebody says the numbers feel a day behind, I believe them and compare the two schedules and their actual durations before looking at anything else."
  - q: "Sync narrow or sync wide?"
    a: "Narrow on objects and fields, because every extra field costs time on every run — but generous with dates, owners, stages, segments and amounts, because a snapshot can only remember what was synced at the time. A field first synced in March has no February values and never will, so the cost of being sparing is paid later and cannot be recovered."
  - q: "What do you check when loading a file?"
    a: "Three things, in order of expense. Dates typed as dates, because a text date renders perfectly in a table and cannot be trended, filtered by range or grouped by month — and fixing it means a reload. Identifiers forced to dimensions, because a numeric-looking ID typed as a measure means the product will offer to sum it and eventually somebody accepts. And currency scale, compared against one raw row by eye, because a value in cents read as whole units is out by a hundred consistently enough that nothing looks anomalous. Then the row count against a known total."
  - q: "Where do bucket boundaries come from?"
    a: "The business — the point at which somebody does something differently. Our under-55% utilisation band exists because that is where a CSM picks up the phone. Splitting a range into even thirds is arithmetic pretending to be judgement, and if nobody can tell me the boundary that is information: the measure may not be actionable yet. I bucket once in a transform node so 'at risk' means the same thing on every screen, and I write down which side the boundary falls on because somebody will eventually land on it."
  - q: "How do you handle missing values?"
    a: "First by deciding whether the blank means 'none' or 'unknown', because every fill is a claim — zero asserts none, the average asserts typical. If I fill, I keep a flag column saying I did, so anybody can exclude imputed rows later and can tell which numbers were real. And I never analyse an imputed value against the thing it was imputed from, because I would find the pattern I put there. Sometimes the missingness is the signal: our blank economic-buyer field predicts losing, not because it causes anything but because a disengaged rep does not fill fields in — genuinely predictive and completely unactionable."
  - q: "Someone says the dataset has duplicates. What do you do?"
    a: "Work out which of three things they mean. Usually it is a join artefact — rows multiplied by a one-to-many join, where the records are fine and the query is wrong, and the fix is the join rather than a de-duplication step. Sometimes it is the intended grain, like an account appearing once per month in a snapshot. Only occasionally is it genuine duplicate records. Deleting rows to fix a fan-out lowers the total so it looks like it worked, while removing real data and leaving the bad join in place for the next measure to hit."
  - q: "How do you debug a recipe producing a wrong number?"
    a: "Monitor first — did it run, when, how many rows — because roughly a third of these end there. Then compare the row count against what the dataset normally holds. Then walk the node previews and find where the count changes unexpectedly, which localises a grain problem in about a minute. Then filter to a single entity and count by hand. The dashboard is last; it usually reports faithfully what it was given."

# Data Preparation: Interview Questions

The questions here are operational. An interviewer is checking whether you have owned a pipeline that runs overnight and been woken up by it.

::note{icon="i-lucide-ear"}
**The answer that marks out experience** is the scheduling one. Everybody can describe sync and recipes; far fewer volunteer that a recipe running before its dependency finishes succeeds every night on stale data with nothing anywhere reporting it. That is a thing you learn by losing a week to it.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"Two clocks — the data is as old as the earlier of sync and recipes."**
2. **"Every fill is a claim, so flag what you filled."**
3. **"Most 'duplicates' are a join artefact; deleting rows makes it look fixed."**
