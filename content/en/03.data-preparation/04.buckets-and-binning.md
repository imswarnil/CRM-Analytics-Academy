# Buckets and Binning

A bucket turns a continuous measure into a category: deal size into Small, Mid and Large; utilisation into At risk, Healthy and Overage; tenure into cohorts. It is a small transform node and a large source of disagreement.

## The boundaries are a business decision

::note{icon="i-lucide-ruler"}
**Even thirds of the range is arithmetic pretending to be judgement.** A band boundary should be the point at which somebody behaves differently. The Academy's under-55% utilisation band exists because that is where a CSM picks up the phone; the over-105% band exists because that is where a renewal conversation changes shape. Both came from how the business operates, not from dividing a range into three, and both are written down beside the metric definitions.
::

If nobody can tell you where the boundary is, that is information: the measure may not yet be actionable, and bucketing it implies a certainty that does not exist.

## Bucket once, in the recipe

::warning{icon="i-lucide-copy"}
**A bucket defined per dashboard becomes several definitions within a year.** "Large deal" means over $100k on the sales dashboard and over $250k on the finance one, both defensible, and the disagreement surfaces in a meeting rather than in a review. A transform node in the recipe produces one banded column that every dashboard inherits — and when the business changes a threshold, it changes in one place.
::

The exception is a band that genuinely belongs to one screen — an exploratory split somebody is trying out. That can live in a query, and it should not survive into something shared.

## The edge cases that cause arguments

::field-table
---
dataset: bucketing decisions to write down
grain: one row per decision
fields:
  - name: "Which side is the boundary on?"
    type: "inclusive or exclusive"
    role: "key"
    note: "Is exactly 55% at risk or healthy? Somebody will eventually land on the boundary and ask, usually in front of other people. Decide, write it in the definition, and be consistent across every band."
  - name: "Where do nulls go?"
    type: "the fourth band"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "A record with no value is not zero and not average. Give it an explicit 'Unknown' band rather than letting it fall into whichever band the formula happens to produce. It is often the most interesting band."
  - name: "Are the bands stable over time?"
    type: "fixed vs relative"
    role: "date"
    note: "Fixed boundaries let you compare periods. Quartile-based bands are always a quarter each, so a band can grow or shrink without anything real changing — which makes period comparison meaningless. Prefer fixed unless you deliberately want relative."
  - name: "Do they cover everything?"
    type: "no gaps"
    role: "measure"
    note: "Bands that stop at 105 leave every account above it uncategorised. The top and bottom bands should be open-ended, or the chart quietly loses rows."
---
::

## Naming the bands

Names are read far more often than boundaries are. "At risk" tells somebody what to do; "Band 1" makes them look up a definition they will not find.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-tag"}
**Name for the action, and keep the numbers in the definition.** "Under-consuming (<55%)" in a legend is ideal: the name carries the meaning, the parenthesis carries the precision, and nobody has to ask what the threshold was. The one thing to avoid is a name that implies a judgement the data does not support — "Failing" rather than "Under-consuming" invites an argument about the account rather than about the number.
::

## Sorting banded columns

A banded column is text, so it sorts alphabetically by default — which puts "At risk" before "Healthy" before "Overage" only by accident, and breaks entirely with numeric names like "10-20" and "100+".

::note{icon="i-lucide-arrow-down-up"}
**Give the bands a sort order in the recipe**, usually a numeric companion column. Otherwise every chart built on them needs the order fixed by hand, and one of them eventually will not be — producing a distribution chart whose bands are in alphabetical order, which reads as noise rather than as a shape.
::
