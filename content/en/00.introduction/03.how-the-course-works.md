# How the Course Works

The whole site is markdown. Every lesson is one file, every file has the same skeleton, and once you recognise the skeleton you can move through a lesson at whatever depth you need.

## The anatomy of a lesson

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "The screen walkthrough"
    description: "A collapsible shot-by-shot script at the top: what to have open, where to click, and what is worth saying about each screen. Until a lesson's video is recorded this IS the video — it is written to be read, not just performed. When a recording lands, the script collapses and stays as the transcript."
  - title: "The lesson"
    description: "Twelve to twenty minutes of prose. Concepts get a diagram or a comparison; mechanics get an exact click path. Anywhere the product has more than one way to do something, the lesson picks one and says why."
  - title: "The Build Brief"
    description: "On hands-on lessons only. Names the dashboard, its audience, the question it answers, the decision it drives, the refresh cadence, the datasets it needs — and what is deliberately NOT on it. The last field is the one that teaches design."
  - title: "The Metric Spec"
    description: "Every number on the build, written out as a formula with its grain, its source and its known trap. This is the artefact you would hand a colleague so two dashboards agree, and the GTM engineering section spends a whole lesson on why it is a contract."
  - title: "The graded quiz"
    description: "Four to six questions, graded in your browser. The answers ship inside the page — this is public open-source markdown, so there is no way to hide them and no pretence otherwise. Only your score is saved, and only if you are signed in."
  - title: "The interview questions"
    description: "One set per section, with model answers, written as a hiring manager would ask them. Answer out loud before you read the answer; recognising a good answer is not the same skill as producing one."
---
::

## One company, nineteen files, every build

Every dataset in the course describes **Namilio**, a fictional company that sells AI infrastructure — voice agents, a messaging API, an LLM endpoint, phone verification, and a studio product. You download its data once and every build in the four go-to-market sections runs on it.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-link"}
**Why one dataset and not a fresh one per lesson.** Because the hard part of analytics is not building a chart, it is that your dashboards have to *agree with each other*. When the demand dashboard says 2,800 leads and the pipeline dashboard says 2,640, somebody has to work out which is right and why — and that argument is the job. A clean toy table per lesson hides it completely. Namilio's data is deliberately built so that argument comes up about six times.
::

## The sixteen builds

Sections 8, 9 and 10 are not lesson sequences, they are build sequences. Each build produces one dashboard in your own org, numbered so you can refer to it later:

- **Builds 1–6**, demand: AI search visibility, organic search, acquisition, regional demand, campaign performance, spend efficiency.
- **Builds 7–9**, pipeline: the lead overview, inbound versus outbound, the lead-to-opportunity handoff, opportunity management, funnel and velocity, CPQ and discounting.
- **Builds 10–14**, RevOps: forecast and commit, the ARR waterfall, consumption and commit risk, service and SLA, and the executive board that ties all thirteen together.

The last one is deliberately the hardest, and not because it has the most widgets. It has the fewest.

## Progress, and what is stored

Signing in is optional. Without it, every lesson, dataset and quiz still works — the whole curriculum is prerendered static pages and nothing is gated.

::note{icon="i-lucide-check-circle-2"}
**If you do sign in,** three things become available: a mark-complete tick at the foot of each lesson, a dashboard showing how far through each section you are, and your quiz scores over time. What is stored is the lesson path, a completion flag, and a score. Not your answers, not your work, and nothing from your Salesforce org — this site never connects to your org, and no lesson ever asks you to give it credentials.
::

## Twelve languages, and which one is real

The site is published in twelve languages. Only **English is written by hand**. The other eleven — Spanish, French, German, Portuguese, Japanese, Chinese, Hindi, Arabic, Russian, Bengali and Urdu — are machine-translated from it by a self-hosted pipeline whenever the English changes.

That is stated plainly because it matters when you are learning: if a translated sentence reads oddly, the English is the source of truth, and switching to it for that paragraph is the right move. Arabic and Urdu render right-to-left. A lesson not yet translated falls back to English rather than showing a blank page.

## When you are stuck

In order, because the order saves time:

1. **Re-read the grain.** Nine out of ten wrong numbers in this course are a join at the wrong grain, and the datasets and modelling section's grain lesson is the fix. If revenue is exactly double, exactly triple, or suspiciously round, it is fan-out.
2. **Check the integration user, then the security predicate.** If rows are *missing* rather than duplicated, this is almost always why. Section 2 covers both.
3. **Compare against the dataset's own README.** `public/sample-data/namilio/README.md` states the true totals — closed revenue, lead count, ARR by month. If your dashboard disagrees with it, the dashboard is wrong.
4. **Search the curriculum** at `/ask`, which finds the lesson that names your error.
5. **Open an issue or a pull request.** Every lesson is a markdown file in a public repo. If something is wrong, unclear, or has been renamed by Salesforce since it was written, the fix is a PR and it will be merged. The contribute page explains how, and the wall of fame credits everyone who has.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-alert-triangle"}
**One thing to know before the Einstein Discovery section.** Salesforce renames this product roughly every three years — Wave, Einstein Analytics, Tableau CRM, CRM Analytics. Older blog posts, Stack Exchange answers and even some official pages use the old names, and the UI still contains a few. They are the same product. Where a name changed, lessons use the current one and mention the old one once.
::
