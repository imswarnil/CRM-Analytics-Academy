# Does the Lead Score Predict Anything?

Every CRM has a lead score. Very few companies have ever checked whether theirs works. This lesson is the check — and at the Academy the answer is *partly*, which is the most useful possible outcome.

::note{icon="i-lucide-gauge"}
**A score is a prediction.** Predictions can be scored against outcomes. If A-band leads do not convert materially better than C-band ones, the score is decoration and reps are right to ignore it.
::

## The lift chart

One chart answers the question. Everything else is diagnosis.

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Group by ScoreBand"
    description: "A, B, C, D already exist in the dataset, derived from LeadScore at 70 / 50 / 30."
  - title: "Plot conversion rate per band"
    description: "count(IsConverted) / count(all) per band. Bars, D through A left to right."
  - title: "Look for monotonic increase"
    description: "Each band should convert better than the one below it. That is the entire test. If the bars are flat or jumbled, the score does not work."
  - title: "Check the volume behind each bar"
    description: "A band with 30 leads and a 40% rate is noise. Add a second series or a label showing lead count per band, or you will act on a rounding error."
  - title: "Measure the actual lift"
    description: "A-band rate divided by overall rate. If A-band converts 2.4× the average, the score has real value and prioritising by it is justified. Under about 1.3× it is not worth the reps' attention."
---
::

::lesson-kpis
---
columns: 4
items:
  - label: "A band (70+)"
    value: "~21.5%"
    trend: flat
    hint: "Convert to opportunity — 1.19× the average"
    spark: [20, 21, 22, 21, 21, 22, 21]
  - label: "B band (50-69)"
    value: "~20.7%"
    hint: "Indistinguishable from A"
  - label: "C band (30-49)"
    value: "~13.6%"
    hint: "Real separation starts here"
  - label: "D band (<30)"
    value: "~1%"
    trend: down
    good: false
    hint: "The one thing the score does well"
---
::

**Read that honestly.** An A-band lift of 1.19× is under the 1.3× line: the score does not tell a rep which of their top leads to call first. What it does do — and this is still worth something — is identify the D band, which converts at about 1% and arguably should not be routed to a human at all. A score whose only real job is exclusion should be described that way, not as a prioritisation model.

## Then find what the score is missing

This is where the lesson earns its place. Test individual fields against conversion and compare them to the score itself.

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Persona"
    subtitle: "A single field"
    icon: "i-lucide-user"
    pros:
      - "Procurement / Finance converts at ~24%, Individual Learner at ~13% — nearly 2×"
      - "Separates converters more sharply than the whole score"
      - "Free: it already exists"
    cons:
      - "Individual Learners are the largest volume, so raw counts mislead"
    verdict: "The strongest single signal in the data. Route on it, and weight it heavily in any rebuild."
  - title: "StartedFreeCourse"
    subtitle: "A behavioural boolean"
    icon: "i-lucide-book-open"
    pros:
      - "Behavioural — they did something, not just matched a profile"
      - "Lifts MQL rate from ~61% to ~70%"
    cons:
      - "Only ~20.5% vs ~17% on conversion to opportunity"
    verdict: "Weaker than expected. Learners are not buyers — the person studying is rarely the person signing."
  - title: "LeadScore"
    subtitle: "The model"
    icon: "i-lucide-sliders"
    pros:
      - "Excludes the D band reliably"
    cons:
      - "No separation between A and B"
      - "Partly built FROM the fields above, so it is not adding much"
    verdict: "Keep it for exclusion, stop presenting it as prioritisation, and recalibrate it on persona."
---
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-lightbulb"}
**The finding most companies get — and the one the Academy gets instead.** Usually a behavioural event outperforms a demographic score, because it is evidence rather than a guess. At a training company the behaviour belongs to the learner and the purchase belongs to their manager, so the demographic field — *who* this person is — carries more signal than *what they did*. Run the audit rather than assuming its result; the right answer depends on who buys.
::

## Two traps in this analysis

::lesson-cards
---
columns: 2
items:
  - title: "The self-fulfilling score"
    icon: "i-lucide-repeat"
    description: "If reps work A-band leads first and hardest, A-band converts better because of the effort, not the score. Control for it by faceting on DaysToFirstTouch and TouchCount — if the lift survives within a single touch-count bucket, it is real."
  - title: "Survivorship"
    icon: "i-lucide-filter-x"
    description: "D-band leads may never be routed at all, so their conversion rate reflects neglect rather than quality. Note which bands actually got worked, and say so beside the chart."
---
::

::note{icon="i-lucide-scale"}
**Both traps push the same way** — they inflate the apparent value of the score. An audit that ignores them will always conclude the model is excellent. Naming them, even without fully resolving them, is what makes the audit trustworthy.
::
