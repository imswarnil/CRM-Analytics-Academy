interview:
  - q: "What is a lens?"
    a: "One exploration of one dataset — a grouping, a measure, a filter, a chart — and it is a disposable workspace rather than a deliverable. I expect to make twenty in an hour and keep none. A dashboard is the opposite: assembled from many queries, faceted together, and built for other people."
  - q: "How is SAQL different from SQL?"
    a: "It is a stream transformation. Each statement takes the previous statement's result and transforms it, so statement order is literally the query plan — there is no optimiser reordering it for me. Practically that means filtering early rather than late, because filtering after grouping means aggregating rows I was about to discard. The other difference that bites is quoting: single quotes for field names, double quotes for string values."
  - q: "A chart shows a number you did not expect. What do you do?"
    a: "Switch to table mode and look at the rows, before anything else. A surprising chart is either an insight or a grain problem, and the rows tell me which in about ten seconds — usually by showing me the same deal appearing twice. Then I read the SAQL to confirm the query is asking what I think I clicked. Changing the chart type tells me nothing."
  - q: "When would you hand-write SAQL rather than click?"
    a: "When the click interface genuinely cannot express it: share of total, a ratio compared across groups, a period-over-period comparison in one query. I still start by clicking the closest approximation and editing the generated query, because that gives me correct field references and quoting for free. What I avoid is defining a business metric in a widget's SAQL — that hides the definition somewhere nobody will find it, and two dashboards will drift apart with neither being wrong."
  - q: "How do you make a dataset usable by other people?"
    a: "Field metadata, before sharing rather than after. Business labels rather than API names, formats set once at the dataset, sensible default aggregations — especially on rates, because a utilisation percentage defaulting to sum reads as 960% across a year — and hide the plumbing so the field list is fifteen meaningful items rather than sixty with keys among them. And the grain in the description."
  - q: "What do you think of natural-language querying?"
    a: "Genuinely useful for getting to a first chart and for people who will never learn the explorer. The failure mode is that it answers fluently even when it has answered a subtly different question — the right measure cohorted on the wrong date, or a calendar quarter when the company runs a different fiscal one. So I treat a result as a starting point to verify in table mode, not as an answer to quote. Its quality also depends almost entirely on field metadata, so most complaints that it does not understand the data are really complaints about labels."
  - q: "What does 'count of rows' mean?"
    a: "Exactly what it says, which is the trap. At line-item grain Namilio's 1,378 rows are 677 opportunities, so counting rows counts lines. If the dataset's grain is finer than the entity I am counting, I use a distinct count on that entity's key — and I check it against a number I already know."

# Lenses and Explorations: Interview Questions

Less frequently probed than grain, and a good place to sound like a practitioner rather than a student.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The single strongest signal in this section** is saying "I'd check it in table mode" in answer to a question about a surprising number. It says you have been wrong before, in public, and built a habit in response. Almost nobody volunteers it, and every experienced person recognises it immediately.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"A lens is a disposable workspace; a dashboard is a deliverable."**
2. **"SAQL is a stream transformation, so statement order is the query plan — filter early."**
3. **"Surprising chart? Table mode first."**
