# Access Is Where Projects Stall

This is the shortest section in the course and the one most people skip. It is also the one that decides whether your work reaches anybody.

::note{icon="i-lucide-key-round"}
**The pattern is always the same.** Week one: someone gets an org, builds a dataset, builds a dashboard, and it works. Week three: it goes to the sales team and it is empty, or it shows them everything including other regions' deals. Week four is spent unpicking a security model that should have been designed before the first dataset existed — because the predicate depends on fields that have to be *synced*, and sometimes on a recipe node that has to *exist*.
::

## Four things, in dependency order

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "Licences"
    description: "What the org is entitled to at all. CRM Analytics comes as its own licence — Growth or Plus — and Einstein Discovery lives in the higher tier. No amount of permission-set work conjures an entitlement the org does not have."
  - title: "Permission sets"
    description: "Who gets to do what. Access is granted by permission set, not by profile, and not by being a System Administrator — which surprises nearly everyone on their first day."
  - title: "The two special users"
    description: "The integration user, which data sync runs as and which therefore decides what data exists at all; and the security user, which is how Salesforce sharing gets evaluated for analytics."
  - title: "Row-level security"
    description: "Which rows each viewer sees, through a security predicate or through sharing inheritance. This is the part that must be designed before the data model, not after."
---
::

## The two failure symptoms, and what each means

You will diagnose these for the rest of your career, so learn them as a pair:

::compare-cols
---
items:
  - title: "Rows are MISSING"
    subtitle: "A user sees less than they should"
    icon: "i-lucide-eye-off"
    pros:
      - "Almost always permissions, not the dashboard"
      - "Check in order: integration user field access, then the predicate, then sharing inheritance"
      - "Then the recipe, for an inner join that dropped unmatched rows"
    cons:
      - "Silent — nothing errors, and the dashboard looks fine to whoever built it"
    verdict: "Missing means permissions. Start upstream."
  - title: "Rows are DUPLICATED"
    subtitle: "A total is 2×, 3×, or suspiciously round"
    icon: "i-lucide-copy"
    pros:
      - "Almost always a join at the wrong grain"
      - "Reproduce it by counting rows before and after the join node"
    cons:
      - "Also silent, and the individual numbers all look plausible"
    verdict: "Duplicated means a join. Stay in the recipe."
---
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-user-check"}
**The habit worth forming now.** Never sign off a dashboard from your own account. An administrator sees everything, so every security mistake is invisible to you specifically. Log in as a restricted user — or use the preview-as feature where available — before you call anything finished. This single habit prevents the most embarrassing category of analytics failure.
::

## Why this is genuinely interesting

It is easy to read this section as administrative overhead. It is actually the product's strongest argument.

Your Salesforce org already encodes, in detail, who may see which records: roles, the hierarchy, sharing rules, territories, teams, manual shares. That model took years and it is enforced on every record. CRM Analytics can *apply* it to analytics rather than making you rebuild it as row-level filters in a second tool — where it will drift, silently, every time the business reorganises.

That is why the section exists here rather than in an appendix. Being the person who can wire this correctly is a large part of being valuable on a CRM Analytics project.
