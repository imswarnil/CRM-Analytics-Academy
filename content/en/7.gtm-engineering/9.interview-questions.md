description: "Rehearse the GTM analytics interview — grain and fan-out, metric definitions, usage versus seat retention, leading indicators, and how to push back on a dashboard request without sounding obstructive."
interview:
  - q: "We sell a usage-based API. How would you measure retention?"
    a: "I'd ask which product first, because the answer differs by pricing model and a single blended number would hide the interesting part. A seat product like Studio retains on a renewal date, so classic gross and net retention work. A usage product is re-earned every day — a customer can shrink 40% without churning, so I'd measure retention as consumption against the prior period and against their commitment, using a monthly snapshot per account per product. A flat platform fee is binary. So I'd build three views and a blended one, and I'd label the blend clearly rather than let it become the headline. The practical reason this matters: on usage revenue, churn is a slow fade rather than an event, and a dashboard that only watches contract status sees nothing until renewal, by which point you've lost two quarters of warning."
  - q: "You join an account table to a monthly usage table and revenue reads ten times too high. What happened?"
    a: "Fan-out. The account table is one row per account and the usage table is one row per account per product per month, so after the join each account appears once per usage row — up to 54 times for an account with three products across eighteen months. The ARR column isn't wrong on any individual row, it's repeated, and summing it multiplies. The fix is to aggregate the usage side to account grain first — sum billed revenue, average utilisation, take the latest commit state — and then join, which keeps the output at one row per account. The habit that catches it is asserting the row count after every join: if the account dataset doesn't come out at the number of accounts I started with, I fanned out. I also keep a couple of anchor totals memorised so an absurd number is obvious at a glance."
  - q: "Marketing reports 1,870 MQLs, sales reports 1,240. Which one is wrong?"
    a: "Probably neither. That gap is almost always a definition difference rather than a computation error, and the most common cause is the date field: marketing counts by the date the lead was created, sales counts by the date it was qualified, and with a lag of up to three weeks the monthly buckets never agree. Other candidates are whether disqualified leads are excluded, whether self-serve signups count, and whether one side is deduplicating by company. My move is not to find the error but to put both definitions side by side, state which date field and which filters each uses, and ask the business which one goes on the board deck. That converts it from a data problem into a business decision, which is where it belongs — and then I write the chosen definition onto the face of the dashboard so it doesn't recur."
  - q: "What's the difference between a leading and a lagging indicator here, and why do you care?"
    a: "ARR is lagging: with a 118-day enterprise cycle, today's closed-won reflects demand from four months ago, so by the time the number moves the cause is cold and there's nothing to act on. Leading indicators sit upstream — share of voice in assistant answers, docs traffic, signup-to-first-API-call, speed-to-lead, stage conversion rates. I care because they're the only place intervention is still possible. The practical consequence is that I watch ratios rather than volumes: volume moves with spend and seasonality, but a conversion rate moves when something actually broke. If speed-to-lead slips from under a day to four days, lead volume looks unchanged and marketing looks fine, and pipeline dips a month later and gets blamed on demand. Only the ratio caught it."
  - q: "A VP asks for revenue by search keyword. How do you respond?"
    a: "I'd say I can't do that honestly and explain why in one sentence: the session that read the page was anonymous, there's no identifier linking it to the account, and the deal closed three or four months later through a different channel. Then I'd offer the nearest true thing — which keyword clusters precede a signup, and a modelled influenced-pipeline view with the assumption written on the chart. I try never to just refuse, because the VP has a real question underneath, usually 'is content worth the budget'. Naming what they asked for, saying plainly what makes it unmeasurable, and putting an honest alternative next to it usually gets taken. If they insist on the first version anyway, it now ships with a stated assumption and they own it, which is the right place for it."
  - q: "How do you decide whether a dashboard is worth building?"
    a: "Four questions before I open the canvas: who specifically opens it, when, what single question it answers, and what they do differently when the number is bad. The fourth is the one that decides it. If nobody can name the decision that changes, the dashboard will get opened twice and abandoned no matter how good it looks, and I've wasted a week. When a request fails that test I usually find the real dashboard is a different one — 'show me leads by source' is often really 'should I move budget', which is a spend-efficiency build. I'd also rather ship six widgets that change a decision than forty that document a department, because every widget I remove makes the rest easier to read."
  - q: "Why would you store an ARR snapshot rather than compute ARR on the fly?"
    a: "Because the moment someone asks what ARR was as at March, a table that only knows today's state cannot answer, and reconstructing it means guessing at which contracts had started and which had lapsed. A monthly snapshot per account, with the movement type that explains the change — new, expansion, contraction, churn — makes the revenue waterfall and net revenue retention straightforward, and it makes historical numbers stable so a chart you showed the board last quarter still says the same thing. The cost is a scheduled job and the discipline not to backfill it. That's a good trade: history that moves is worse than no history, because people notice and stop trusting everything else you publish."
  - q: "Our win rate is 33%. What would you want to know before believing it?"
    a: "The denominator, and whether it's segmented. Win rate should be closed-won over closed deals only; if open opportunities are in the denominator it's counting live pipeline as losses and understating the rate, which is the most common version of the error. Then I'd split it, because a blended rate across segments that differ by a hundred times in deal size describes no actual deal — at Namilio self-serve wins about 44% and strategic about 24%, so 33% is an artefact of the mix rather than a fact about selling. I'd also separate new business from renewal, since renewals win around 88% and quietly inflate any combined figure. And I'd check whether deals lost on 'timing' come back later as new opportunities, because counting the same demand as both a loss and a new lead distorts both numbers."
  - q: "Where does a self-serve motion break a standard funnel report?"
    a: "In three places. Sequence: a developer reads the docs, signs up and calls the API before anyone knows their name, so usage precedes qualification and the 'funnel' is really a loop with a sales motion bolted onto part of it. Definition: a large share of self-serve signups never pay, so 'customer count' needs a rule before it can be charted, and finance, marketing and the board will each want a different one. And expansion: at a usage company a lot of growth arrives with no session, no lead and no opportunity — an account simply doubles its volume — so a funnel-only view misses the single largest source of net new revenue. I keep self-serve in the dataset rather than filtering it out, because it's where most enterprise logos originate two years earlier."
  - q: "How would you tell whether support is costing the company revenue?"
    a: "I'd join cases to accounts and then to the ARR snapshot, which is possible because both carry the account key, and look at whether accounts with SLA breaches or repeated escalations show contraction or churn in the following months rather than immediately. Then I'd bring in platform health, because I need to separate 'we had a real incident' from 'we got slower at answering' — a spike in cases with a matching error-rate spike is an engineering problem, and the same spike with flat platform metrics is a staffing or documentation problem, and those have different owners. I'd also treat CSAT carefully: only about 42% of closed cases get a response, and unhappy customers answer more often, so the average overstates dissatisfaction and I'd show the response rate beside it."

# GTM Engineering: Interview Questions

Ten questions covering this module. The pattern in the strong answers is the same throughout: **name the ambiguity before answering the question**.

::note{icon="i-lucide-mic"}
Most of these are asked to see whether you will pick a definition silently. The best answer usually starts by asking which of two or three reasonable things the interviewer means — then answers all of them briefly.
::

::tip{icon="i-lucide-check-circle-2"}
**The three moves that carry a GTM analytics interview.** Say the grain out loud before you join anything. Separate the metric from its definition, and hand the definition back to the business. Prefer a ratio to a volume when you are asked how you would monitor something.
::
