# Preparing Data for a Model

Every mistake in this lesson produces a model that reports good accuracy and is worthless. That combination is what makes preparation the part worth slowing down for.

## One row per thing you will score

::note{icon="i-lucide-ruler"}
**The grain question again, with higher stakes.** If you want to score opportunities, the training data is one row per opportunity. Join in line items and the same deal appears three times, weighting big multi-product deals three times as heavily in training — and the model will be quietly biased towards whatever those deals have in common. A chart at the wrong grain is visibly wrong; a model at the wrong grain is confidently plausible.
::

## Choosing the outcome

The outcome column is the question. Getting it wrong is not recoverable by better modelling.

::field-table
---
dataset: outcome traps
grain: one row per trap
fields:
  - name: "Mixed populations"
    type: "the most common"
    role: "key"
    note: "The Academy's opportunities include renewals that win 77% of the time. Train on all Closed Won and the model learns that the best predictor of winning is being an existing customer — true, useless, and it will report excellent accuracy while doing it. Filter to new business, or model the two separately."
  - name: "An outcome that moves"
    type: "ambiguous"
    role: "date"
    note: "'Churned' for a team plan is usually a fade in seat usage with no cancellation event. You have to define it — three months below a threshold, say — and that definition IS the model's question. Write it down; somebody will assume a different one."
  - name: "Too rare"
    type: "class imbalance"
    role: "measure"
    note: "If 3% of records have the outcome, a model that always predicts 'no' is 97% accurate and completely useless. Accuracy is the wrong measure here, and the story's own headline figure will flatter it."
  - name: "Defined after the fact"
    type: "leakage's cousin"
    role: "measure"
    note: "An outcome computed from fields that only exist once the outcome is known. Check that every input would have been available at the moment you want a prediction."
---
::

## Leakage

The single most common reason a model looks brilliant and is worthless.

::warning{icon="i-lucide-droplet"}
**Leakage is a field recorded at or after the outcome.** `ClosedDate`, `ContractSignedDate`, `LossReason`, a stage that only exists post-decision — each predicts the outcome nearly perfectly, and each is unavailable at the moment you would actually want a score. The tell is an accuracy figure that feels too good. The fix is to ask of every column: *would this have been populated, with this value, at the point of prediction?* If not, remove it.
::

The Academy's pipeline data has several: `LossReason` obviously, and more subtly the stage-entry dates for stages that only occur in won deals.

## Time, and training on the future

A model trained on all of history and evaluated on the same rows will look excellent and tell you nothing about next quarter. Train on earlier data and evaluate on later data, mirroring how it will be used: predicting forward from a point in time.

## Missing values are a decision

Blank is not zero and it is not neutral. Filling a blank amount with zero asserts a deal is worth nothing; filling with the average asserts it is typical. Both are claims, and they change what the model learns.

::tip{icon="i-lucide-question-mark"}
**Missingness is sometimes the signal.** At the Academy, `EconomicBuyerIdentified` being blank may correlate with losing — not because the blank causes the loss, but because a rep who is not engaged does not fill fields in. That makes "is it blank" a genuinely predictive feature and a completely unactionable one, and it is exactly the kind of factor to notice before it reaches a rep as advice.
::
