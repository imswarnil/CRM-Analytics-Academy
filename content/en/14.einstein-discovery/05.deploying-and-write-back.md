# Deploying and Write-Back

A deployed model scores records and writes the result back onto the Salesforce record — which is the moment it stops being analysis and starts influencing behaviour.

## Put it where the work is

::note{icon="i-lucide-map-pin"}
**The strongest thing about write-back is placement.** A score on a dashboard is something a rep has to go and look at; a score on the opportunity they already have open is something they cannot miss. This is the same argument as embedding in the Einstein Discovery section, and it is the difference between a model that changes what people do and one that is admired quarterly and ignored daily.
::

## What travels with the score

Three things, and the second is the one that gets dropped:

::lesson-steps
---
items:
  - title: "The prediction"
    description: "The number or the classification. On its own it is inert — a rep cannot do anything with 23%."
  - title: "The top factors, in plain language"
    description: "The three things pulling it down, phrased as a person would say them. This is the part that makes the score a to-do list rather than a verdict, and it is the part most often omitted because it is more work to surface."
  - title: "The date it was scored"
    description: "A prediction from six weeks ago on a deal that has moved twice since is worse than no prediction. If the score is stale, say so where it is displayed."
---
::

::warning{icon="i-lucide-bell-off"}
**A score with no reasons is ignored within a month, and then distrusted.** The sequence is predictable: it arrives, people look at it, it does not tell them what to do, they stop looking, and when somebody later asks why the AI investment did not change anything the answer is that it never gave anyone an action. Surfacing the factors is not polish — it is the deployment.
::

## Scoring cadence

Records change. Decide deliberately how often they are re-scored, and make the choice visible:

- **On refresh** — scores follow the dataset's schedule. Simple, and a deal that moved this morning still carries last night's score.
- **On change** — closer to real time and more expensive; worth it only when somebody acts on the score within the day.

Either is defensible. What is not defensible is nobody knowing which one is in place, because then nobody knows how much to trust a score that disagrees with the record in front of them.

## Monitoring

::field-table
---
dataset: what to watch after deployment
grain: one row per check
fields:
  - name: "Does it still predict?"
    type: "monthly"
    role: "key"
    note: "Compare predictions against what actually happened. A model trained before a new product launched has a view of the old portfolio, and decay is the normal condition rather than a defect."
  - name: "Does anybody act on it?"
    type: "the adoption check"
    role: "dimension"
    note: "A model everybody ignores is a maintenance cost with no return. This is measurable — do low-scored deals get worked differently — and it is almost never measured."
  - name: "Has the input data drifted?"
    type: "quietly fatal"
    role: "measure"
    note: "A field that stopped being populated, a picklist that gained values, a segment that was renamed. The model keeps scoring, confidently, on inputs that no longer mean what they meant."
  - name: "Is it changing the data it learns from?"
    type: "the feedback loop"
    role: "measure"
    note: "If reps fill in a field because the model rewards it, that field stops measuring what it measured. The model degrades precisely because it was used, and only a periodic re-examination catches it."
---
::

## The retirement plan

::tip{icon="i-lucide-power-off"}
**Decide, at deployment, what would make you turn it off.** Accuracy below a stated threshold, a portfolio change large enough that the training data no longer describes the business, or simply nobody acting on it for a quarter. A model with no retirement condition never gets retired — it just gets quietly stopped being believed, which is worse, because it stays on the record page looking authoritative.
::
