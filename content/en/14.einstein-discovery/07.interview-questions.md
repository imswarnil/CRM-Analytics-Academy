interview:
  - q: "What is the difference between a story and a model?"
    a: "A story is the analysis: what correlates with an outcome, in what direction and how strongly. It produces no scores. A model is a deployed story — it scores live records and can write the prediction back onto the Salesforce record, which means it starts influencing what people do. The gap between running a story and deploying a model is where the judgement lives."
  - q: "What must ship with a deployed prediction?"
    a: "Its top factors, in plain language, and the date it was scored. A bare probability gives a rep nothing to act on, so they stop looking within a month, and then when somebody asks why the investment changed nothing the answer is that it never produced an action. '23% likely to close' is inert; 'no economic buyer, 47 days since last activity, discount above 30%' is a to-do list."
  - q: "What is leakage and how do you catch it?"
    a: "A field recorded at or after the outcome — closed date, loss reason, a stage that only exists post-decision. It predicts the outcome nearly perfectly and is unavailable at the moment you would want a prediction. The tell is an accuracy figure that feels too good. The check is to ask of every column whether it would have been populated, with that value, at the point of prediction."
  - q: "A model reports 97% accuracy. Are you impressed?"
    a: "Not until I know the base rate. If 3% of leads convert, always predicting 'no' is 97% accurate and useless. The first question about any accuracy figure is what a trivial baseline would score — always predicting the majority class — and then what the model gets right that guessing would not."
  - q: "How would you prepare Namilio's opportunity data for a win model?"
    a: "One row per opportunity, because that is what I want to score — joining line items triples multi-product deals and biases the training silently. Filter to new business, because renewals win 88% of the time and including them teaches the model that the best predictor of winning is already being a customer: true, useless, and it reports excellent accuracy while doing it. Then remove leaked fields, check the class balance, and train on earlier data evaluated on later data so it mirrors predicting forward."
  - q: "How do you decide whether a story is worth deploying?"
    a: "I write the sentence: when this score is low, this person will do this differently. If I cannot fill it in with a real person and a real action, the story is worth reading and the model is not worth deploying. Most stories have top factors that are descriptive — segment, industry, company size — which are informative and unactionable, and a model made entirely of those changes nothing."
  - q: "What do you monitor after deployment?"
    a: "Four things. Whether it still predicts, by comparing predictions against what happened — decay is normal, because the business moves and a model trained before a product launched has a view of the old portfolio. Whether anyone acts on it, which is measurable and almost never measured. Whether the inputs have drifted. And whether using it is changing the data it learns from: if reps fill a field because the model rewards it, that field stops measuring engagement and starts measuring compliance. I also set a retirement condition at deployment, because a model with no retirement condition is never retired, it is just quietly stopped being believed while it keeps looking authoritative on the record page."
  - q: "Your company has a lead score nobody has validated. What do you do?"
    a: "Audit it, in about an hour. Bucket the score, join what actually happened after it was assigned, plot conversion rate by band with the volumes beside the rates, and compare against a trivial baseline like lead source. Three outcomes: it climbs, which is good; it climbs then flattens, which is the common one and means it separates bad from average while saying nothing about the top — where the team spends its time; or it is flat, which means it is noise. The hard part is not analytical, it is that the score has been routing people's work for years, so I lead with the method and let the chart make the argument."

# Einstein Discovery: Interview Questions

This section separates people who have trained a model from people who have shipped one. Training is a day; shipping means placement, explanation, monitoring and eventually retirement.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The answer that marks out experience is the audit.** Volunteering that you would test an existing score against what actually happened — with the trivial-baseline comparison and the note that the difficulty is political rather than technical — describes a specific thing you have done and that most candidates have never considered doing.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"A prediction ships with its reasons, or it does not ship."**
2. **"Check the base rate before being impressed by accuracy."**
3. **"A score is a claim, and claims can be tested against what happened."**
