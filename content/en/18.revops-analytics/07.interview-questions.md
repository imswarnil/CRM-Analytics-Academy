interview:
  - q: "What is net revenue retention and why does it matter more than new business?"
    a: "NRR is what happened to the revenue from customers you already had: opening ARR plus expansion minus contraction minus churn, divided by opening, over the cohort present at the start of the period. New business is excluded deliberately — include it and you are measuring sales, not retention. It matters more in a usage business because most growth arrives without a salesperson: an account consuming more than it committed to expands with no opportunity, no session and no lead. And it compounds, which new business does not."
  - q: "NRR is 112%. What else do you need to know?"
    a: "Gross retention, and the split by segment. NRR of 112 with gross of 108 is a healthy base; NRR of 112 with gross of 94 is a bucket losing six points a period, refilled by a handful of expanding accounts, and one of those churning flips the picture. The two have identical headlines and completely different futures, which is why I put gross beside NRR permanently. Then segment, because a blended figure lets one bleeding segment hide behind another's growth for quarters."
  - q: "How do you predict churn when there is no cancellation event?"
    a: "Consumption trend. In a usage business churn is a four-month fade — no cancellation record, no closed-lost, no alert — so contract status finds out at the renewal date. I flag accounts whose trailing utilisation is under the business's threshold AND falling, because level alone produces a list full of accounts that bought too much once and are not leaving. Then I join ARR to it, because eleven accounts is a number and $2.1M of ARR is a decision, and it is also what makes the list sortable in the order that matters."
  - q: "Why never average a utilisation percentage?"
    a: "It weights an account with twenty thousand committed units the same as one with twenty-one million. The correct figure is sum of used divided by sum of committed. It is the average-of-ratios trap, and in this data the two numbers differ enough to change what you would do. I also never roll utilisation across products with different units — messages and seats do not add."
  - q: "Is overage revenue good news?"
    a: "It is revenue and a risk at the same time, and I report it next to the renewal date for that reason. Sustained overage is unbudgeted spend on the customer's side, so it surfaces in their procurement review, and the usual outcome is a renegotiated unit price at renewal. Celebrating it in isolation sets up a surprise two quarters later."
  - q: "How would you measure AI deflection honestly?"
    a: "Net of reopens. A deflected case that came back was not deflected, it was delayed, and it cost the customer an extra round trip. I report gross and net side by side so nobody suspects the gap is being hidden. Reporting gross alone is the flattering version, and it is the version that gets an AI investment renewed on a false premise and then blamed when support load does not fall."
  - q: "Support says a SEV1 caused a churn. How would you check?"
    a: "Both tables carry AccountId, so it is provable rather than anecdotal. Take accounts with the churn-risk link on their cases and look at their ARR movements in the following months in the snapshot — contraction or churn appearing after the incident. I would also check the other direction, the accounts with SEV1s that did not contract, because a claim that only looks at confirming cases is not evidence."
  - q: "You have thirteen dashboards. How do you build the executive board?"
    a: "By deciding what comes off. Roughly 120 widgets exist and nine things survive, and the work is not picking the nine — most people pick a similar nine — it is defending the refusals to the people who built them. My test per number is whether a twenty percent move would change a decision this quarter, and every chart on the board has to explain one of the tiles above it. Anything that needs a paragraph to interpret will be misread in a glance, so it belongs on its own dashboard with a link."
  - q: "How do you handle a CSAT score with a 42% response rate?"
    a: "Publish the response rate beside it, permanently. The average is not wrong, it just answers a narrower question than its label claims — it describes people who chose to respond, and response skews to the delighted and the furious. A satisfaction score without a response rate is an advertisement; with one it is a measurement with stated limits, and stating the limit is what makes the rest of the dashboard credible."

# RevOps and Retention: Interview Questions

This section's questions are the ones that most distinguish a senior candidate, because they are about measuring honestly when a flattering number is available and easier to produce.

::note{icon="i-lucide-star"}
**The strongest single answer here is the NRR follow-up.** Asked "NRR is 112%", the junior answer is "that's good". The senior answer asks for gross retention and the segment split, then explains why two identical headlines can describe opposite situations. It demonstrates that you have watched a blended number hide a real problem, which is the experience the question is fishing for.
::

## The three sentences worth memorising

1. **"NRR excludes new business, or you are measuring sales rather than retention."**
2. **"In a usage business, churn is a trend, not an event."**
3. **"Report the response rate beside the satisfaction score."**
